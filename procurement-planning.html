Example · Procurement planning

A new forecast just landed. What do we actually need to buy?

A change in demand for one product spreads through hundreds of materials. This page shows how the procurement planning simulator works out exactly which raw-material orders are needed, and when, how much and why. It does this in a safe copy of the plan, so SAP stays untouched.

Before · How a forecast change is handled today

New forecast, new orders. Without checking.

When a new forecast arrives, the safe assumption is to order all the raw materials it needs as new. Nobody can check first what is already on order, or what the plan actually needs.

01 · The habit

Order everything new.

Every raw material for the extra demand is ordered fresh, even when open SAP orders could cover part of it if they arrived a little earlier.

Risk: more stock and cash tied up than needed

02 · The limitation

SAP has no playground.

Running MRP creates real planned orders and requisitions. To try a scenario you would have to create the orders, then delete them if you didn't like the result.

Risk: what-if questions stay unanswered

03 · The blind spot

The knock-on effect is invisible.

One finished good pulls on sub-assemblies, which pull on purchased materials, sometimes nine levels deep and shared with other products.

Risk: shortages and late orders found too late

The question planners need answered: "What exactly do I need to order, when, how much, and why?"

After · The simulator

A safe copy of the real plan.

The simulator rebuilds SAP's plan from today's stock and every open order and demand. It then plans the new demand the way SAP MRP would. You can try any scenario, and nothing has to be created or deleted in SAP.

Step 1 · Follow the demand down

One forecast change, hundreds of materials.

Follow one forecast change for Product A, an example finished good.

  1. Upload: demand for Product A goes up 25% for Q1 2027.
  2. Production: stock of Product A would drop below safety stock, so the simulator plans 20 production orders, e.g. 1,800 units in January, 1,200 in February, 900 in March.
  3. Sub-assemblies: each production order needs components. Their own stock is checked, and new production orders follow level by level: 1,300 in this run.
  4. Purchased materials: the demand reaches 400 materials, including Material B. Products that share a material are planned together, so it sees their combined demand.

Step 2 · Use what's already ordered

Pull forward first. Buy new only for the rest.

Here is what happens to Material B, a purchased material. The simulator checks each day whether stock stays above safety stock.

  1. The shortage: January production of Product A needs 6,100 units of Material B on 1 Jan. Stock would fall to −3,500, far below the 1,500 safety stock.
  2. Pull forward: an open SAP purchase order for 3,000 units is due 12 Jan, within Material B's delivery time. The simulator pulls it forward to 1 Jan, using its exact SAP order number.
  3. Buy only the rest: 2,000 units are still missing, so one new purchase order is planned, rounded up to the 5,000 minimum lot. Stock is back above safety stock.
  4. Across the run: 3,000 existing orders pulled forward, and only 2,200 new purchase orders needed. More gaps were closed with orders already in SAP than with new ones.

Step 3 · When and how much

The right date and quantity, by SAP's own rules.

Each new order uses the company's master data for the material: planned delivery time, safety stock, minimum lot size and rounding quantity. The simulator applies these rules the way SAP MRP does.

When to order

Order date = need date − planned delivery time.

Material B is needed on 1 Jan 2027 and has a 21-day delivery time, so it must be ordered by 11 Dec 2026. If that date is already past, the order is flagged late: order now and expedite.

250 new purchase orders in the run were already late. They are the first ones to act on.

How much to order

Shortfall, then minimum lot, then rounding.

The quantity starts with what's needed to get back to safety stock. It is raised to the minimum lot size, then rounded up to the rounding quantity. If that pushes stock above the target stock level, it is listed as an exception.

Step 4 · Why it's needed

Every order can be traced back to its demand.

Each simulated order is linked to the finished good and the demand it serves. A buyer can see why an order exists before placing it, and tell the forecast's orders apart from shortages that were already in SAP's plan.

Caused by the forecastAt least part of the order serves the new demand. These orders only exist because of the forecast change.
Already short in SAP's planThe material would be short even without the forecast change. It needs attention either way.

Why you can trust it

Built on SAP's plan. Checked every run.

A simulation is only useful if planners believe it. Four built-in safeguards make the results checkable.

01

Starts from SAP's own plan.

Today's stock and every open order and demand are rebuilt from SAP. The rebuilt plan matched SAP's projected stock for all materials: 0 mismatches.

02

Self-checked on every run.

Before planning, the engine is tested on 4 hand-checked cases and 60 random cases against a separate, simpler MRP calculation. If any case fails, the run stops.

03

Never changes SAP.

It writes results to the Celonis dashboard only. Existing SAP orders are never changed; pull-forwards are proposals with the exact SAP order number.

04

Says what it can't solve.

Gaps are listed, not hidden. In this run that meant 300 exceptions: late orders, missing master data, and a finished good with no bill of materials.

How to use it

Ask the question. Get the orders.

Anyone can run it. It is built for questions where planners need to see the effect on purchasing before committing to anything in SAP.

The routine

  1. Upload the new demand for the finished goods in Excel: product, quantity, date.
  2. Press the button in Celonis. Results arrive in about 30 minutes.
  3. Start with what's late, then pull-forwards, then new purchase orders.
  4. Check the why before acting: which product and demand each order serves.
  5. Act in SAP: reschedule the existing orders, create only the new orders that are needed, and fix the exceptions.
