Example · Supply resilience

If one supplier stopped tomorrow, which part would hurt most?

A manufacturer buys tens of thousands of parts. Most could slip for a week and nobody would notice. A few would stop production lines within days. This page explains how the daily resilience ranking finds those few, and what to do when a part reaches the top.

From measuring the loss to preventing it

Business impact sizes the loss. The ranking sees it coming.

Most planning teams already track a Business impact KPI. It shows how much value flows through a part, but not whether that supply is about to fail.

Business impact = 12-month future supply of the component × average invoiced sales price per unit over the last 12 months

How it works · 4 checks per part

Every part gets the same four questions.

Here they are for one example part from the ranking.

A Part A: a purchased component Rank 3 of 35,000 in today's ranking
  1. Reach: how many finished products need it? About 150 finished products depend on Part A.
  2. Supplier: is there an alternative? One supplier delivers 100% of the volume.
  3. Deliveries: are receipts behaving normally? Delivery times swing by about ±70 days, well outside a healthy range. The model treats this as an early sign the supplier may be in trouble.
  4. Survive vs. recover: can stock last until supply is back? Time to survive is about 10 days; time to recover is about 300 days.

The key test · Survive vs. recover

Can the stock outlast the recovery?

Two numbers decide whether a supply stop becomes a line stop. The model works them out for every part, every day.

  1. Time to survive: if Part A's supplier stopped today, usable stock above safety stock would last 10 days at current daily consumption.
  2. Time to recover: about 300 days until supply is back. That is ~200 days of normal delivery time, plus ~100 days because there is no second source to switch to. Parts that depend on other parts take longer still.
  3. The gap: 290 days without stock. Part A cannot bridge its own recovery, which is why it ranks so high.
CoveredTime to survive ≥ time to recover. Stock bridges the outage.
ExposedTime to survive < time to recover. The gap is the number of days to bridge.

Outside-in · External risk signals

An outside view of every supplier.

The company's own receipts show what has already happened. External supplier-risk data adds what is happening at the supplier: an overall operational and ESG risk score, and a financial-health and insolvency rating. A supplier only counts as low risk if both say so.

This sets each part's starting likelihood, between 5% and 40%. Delivery history then moves it up or down, receipt by receipt.

How to use it · Daily dashboard

Start at the top. Read the signals. Act.

The ranking refreshes every day in the Celonis dashboard. The score tells you where to look; the signals on each row tell you what to do. Select a signal on Part A's row to see the matching action.

  1. Open the dashboard each morning and filter to your category or plant.
  2. Work from the top down. Check which signals are lit. Where parts rank similarly, tackle the one with the higher business impact first.
  3. Take the matching action and agree who owns it. Tomorrow's ranking shows whether it worked.
